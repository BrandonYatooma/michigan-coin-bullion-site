What Is "Junk Silver"? A Quick Guide to 90% Silver Coins

"Junk silver" is one of the more confusing terms in coin collecting, mostly because it sounds like it means the opposite of what it does. Nothing about it is actually junk — it's simply the industry's nickname for ordinary, circulated U.S. coins that happen to be struck in 90% silver.

Where the Name Comes From

Before 1965, U.S. dimes, quarters, and half dollars were minted in a 90% silver, 10% copper alloy. When silver prices rose well beyond face value, these coins stopped circulating as money and started trading for their metal content instead. Dealers needed a quick way to refer to "common, well-worn silver coins with no collectible value beyond their silver content" — and "junk silver" stuck.

Why It's Priced by Face Value, Not by the Coin

Because a pre-1965 dime, quarter, and half dollar all contain a predictable, proportional amount of silver relative to their denomination, dealers price bags of this coin as a multiple of face value rather than coin by coin. A common shorthand: if you have $1 in face value of pre-1965 U.S. coins (four quarters, or ten dimes, or any combination), that $1 face value is priced at a set multiple — you can see our current buy and sell multiples on our live prices page.

How to Spot It

  • Check the date — anything minted 1964 or earlier is 90% silver (with a few well-known exceptions like 1965–1970 half dollars, which are 40% silver).
  • Look for wear — junk silver is typically well-circulated, not uncirculated or proof condition.
  • No silver color giveaway needed — nearly all pre-1965 dimes, quarters, and half dollars qualify, so date is the fastest check.

What It's Not

A coin with genuine collectible value — rare dates, mint errors, exceptional condition — is worth pursuing as a numismatic item, not melting into "junk" pricing. If you're not sure which category your coins fall into, that's exactly what an in-person evaluation is for.
